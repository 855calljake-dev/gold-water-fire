Local context

What makes Phoenix house fires behave the way they do

Phoenix housing stock spans a wide range, and the era of the house changes what a fire does to it. Central and south Phoenix neighborhoods — Encanto, Coronado, Willo, Garfield — hold a lot of pre-1960 construction: wood lath and plaster, original wood floors, shallow attics, and in many cases knob-and-tube or early cloth-insulated wiring that has been added onto over decades. Smoke in those homes travels through wall cavities and lath gaps and lands in places far from the ignition point. The postwar and 1970s–80s tracts across Maryvale, Alhambra, Sunnyland and much of north Phoenix bring different problems: flat or low-slope roofs, blown-in attic insulation that holds odor like a sponge, and evaporative coolers that pull outside air — and smoke — through the whole house if they are running when a fire starts. Newer construction on the north and far-west edges of the city uses more synthetic materials in carpet pad, cabinetry and framing adhesives, and those burn into an oily, acidic soot that etches aluminum window frames, chrome fixtures and appliance finishes if it sits. Seasonally, Phoenix has two distinct fire pressure points. Summer is the long one: 110-degree stretches from June through August push air conditioning systems, older service panels and extension-cord loads to their limits, and rooftop package units on flat-roof homes fail hot. Then there is the monsoon — July through September lightning and blowing dust, with outflow winds that can knock down service drops and start attic fires. The other spike is around holidays and cool nights when space heaters, fireplaces that have not been swept in years, and outdoor fire pits on covered patios come back into use. Phoenix's dry air is a factor throughout: low humidity means smoke odor bonds fast to porous surfaces, and it also means the drywall and framing soaked by fire hoses can look dry on the surface within a day while staying wet inside the wall.

The work

What fire restoration actually involves after the department leaves

Fire damage is really three problems stacked on each other, and they have to be handled in order. The first is water. Suppression puts hundreds of gallons into a structure, and that water goes down through floor systems and sideways into wall cavities. If it is not extracted and dried, you get microbial growth in the same walls you are about to clean soot off. That is why fire work usually starts with the same equipment and process as water damage restoration — extraction, controlled demolition of unsalvageable materials, and monitored drying. The second is soot and residue. Different fires leave different residues, and they are cleaned differently. Dry, powdery soot from a fast, hot fire is removed mechanically — chemical sponges, HEPA vacuuming, dry cleaning of surfaces — because introducing liquid first smears it into the substrate. Wet, greasy protein residue from a kitchen fire is nearly invisible and clings to cabinet interiors, ceilings and the tops of door casings, and it needs degreasing agents and time. Acidic soot from burned plastics and electronics is corrosive, so metal fixtures, HVAC coils, hinges and appliance housings get attention early before pitting sets in. The third is odor, and it is the one people underestimate. Smoke particles are small enough to enter porous materials — insulation, carpet pad, subfloor, unsealed framing, the paper face of drywall. Cleaning the visible surface does not remove what is inside the material. Real odor work means removing what cannot be cleaned, cleaning and deodorizing the ductwork so the HVAC system stops recirculating the smell, treating remaining surfaces, and in many cases sealing framing and subfloor before anything new goes back on top. If a house still smells like smoke on the first hot afternoon after the work is done, something in that sequence was skipped.

Putting it back

From cleanup to a finished house

Once the structure is dry, clean and odor-free, what is left is construction — drywall, texture, paint, trim, cabinets, flooring, sometimes framing and roof deck. Before we arrive

What to do — and what not to do — in the first hours

Do not re-enter the structure until the fire department clears it. Electrical, gas and structural conditions after a fire are not always visible. Do not run the HVAC system. If the air handler cycles on, it pulls soot through the ducts and distributes it into rooms the fire never reached. Shut it off at the thermostat and at the breaker if you can do so safely. This is especially relevant in Phoenix homes with evaporative coolers, which move a very high volume of outside air through the house. Do not wipe soot off walls, ceilings or upholstery with a damp cloth or household cleaner. On dry soot, wiping presses it into the paint or fabric and turns a cleanable surface into one that has to be replaced. Do take photographs of everything before anything is moved, including closed cabinets and closets. Do open windows if outside conditions allow and the structure has been cleared. Do move undamaged clothing, documents, medications and electronics out of the smoke-affected area, and set aside anything irreplaceable so it can be handled separately. Do call your insurance carrier to open the claim, and write down the claim number and adjuster's name. If there is standing water from suppression, note that in Phoenix's summer heat a closed-up wet house reaches conditions favorable to microbial growth quickly. Time matters more here than the outside air being dry would suggest.

Common Questions

Questions people ask us

Why does my house still smell like smoke after it looks clean?

Because smoke odor lives inside porous materials, not on top of them. Carpet pad, insulation, drywall paper, unsealed framing and subfloor absorb smoke particles, and surface cleaning does not reach them. Ductwork is another common culprit — if the HVAC ran during or after the fire, the system holds residue and redistributes the smell every time it cycles. Lasting odor removal means removing what cannot be cleaned, cleaning the duct system, treating remaining surfaces and sealing framing and subfloor before new finishes go on.

Do I have water damage too, even though the problem was a fire?

Almost always, yes. Suppression water runs down through floor assemblies and into wall cavities, and in Phoenix's dry air the surfaces can feel dry within a day while the framing and drywall behind them are still wet. Fire jobs are handled with extraction and monitored drying first, before soot cleanup, so you are not sealing moisture behind newly cleaned or newly rebuilt walls.

Can smoke damage a room the fire never reached?

Yes. Smoke follows air movement — up stairwells, through return-air pathways, into wall and attic cavities, and through the gaps in older lath-and-plaster construction common in central Phoenix. A running HVAC system or evaporative cooler will move it through the entire house. It is normal to find soot film and odor in rooms that show no fire damage at all.
