What happens in Scottsdale homes

Why fire damage here does not stay in one room

Scottsdale's housing runs a wide range, and the type of house changes how smoke travels through it. The 1960s and 70s ranch homes around Old Town, the Villa Monterey area, and south Scottsdale often have flat or low-slope roofs, ducted evaporative or early central systems, and open plan living areas. Smoke moves laterally and fast, and soot ends up in bedrooms that never saw flame. The 1990s and 2000s two-story stucco homes north of Shea and out through McDowell Mountain Ranch and DC Ranch stack the problem vertically: heat and smoke rise through stairwells and open great rooms, load into the second-floor ceilings, and settle into attic insulation. In both cases the visible burn is often a small part of the actual job. Three other things are specific to this area. Tile roofs and stucco exteriors hide char and heat damage to the sheathing underneath, so a roof that looks intact from the driveway may not be. Homes on the desert edge (north Scottsdale, up against the McDowell Sonoran Preserve, Troon, Pinnacle Peak) carry real brush-fire exposure in the dry months before monsoon, and wind-driven exterior fire behaves differently than a kitchen fire. And in summer, a house left closed up after a fire bakes: heat drives soot residue deeper into drywall, cabinet finishes, and plastics, and odor sets harder the longer it sits.

The work itself

What restoring a fire-damaged house actually involves

Restoration follows a fairly consistent order, and knowing it helps you tell whether the work being done on your house makes sense. First is safety and stabilization: power and gas verified off where needed, structure checked, and the openings boarded or tarped so the house is not open to weather and wildlife. Second is water. Nearly every fire scene is also a water scene, because suppression water and broken lines soak drywall, insulation, and flooring, and in Arizona summer, wet material inside a hot house grows mold quickly. That water has to be extracted and the structure dried before anything else. Third is removal of what cannot be cleaned: charred framing and sheathing, saturated insulation, burned cabinetry, ductwork that has drawn soot through the whole system. Fourth is cleaning the surfaces that stay. Soot is not one substance. A protein fire from cooking leaves a thin, sharp-smelling film that is nearly invisible until it is disturbed, while burning plastics and synthetics leave an oily black residue that smears if it is wiped wrong. They take different chemistry and different methods, and using the wrong one grinds residue into the finish permanently. Fifth is odor. Smoke odor lives in porous material (insulation, carpet pad, unsealed framing, drywall paper), and masking it does nothing. It is handled by removing the sources, cleaning what remains, treating and sealing surfaces, and running air treatment until the air itself tests clean. Only then does rebuilding start.

Common Questions

Questions people ask us

Should I go back into the house to get my things?

Wait until the structure has been cleared as safe. After that, be careful about what you handle. Soot on a surface is easy to grind into the finish with a rag, and items that look fine can be carrying odor and residue that need specific cleaning. Photograph what you can before anything is moved, and let the restoration crew know which items matter most to you so they are handled first and separately.

Why is there so much water damage when the fire was in one room?

Suppression water spreads far beyond the burn area. It runs along floors, down inside wall cavities, and through ceilings to the level below. Heat also breaks supply lines and sprinkler heads. In Arizona summer especially, that wet material inside a closed-up house is a mold problem within days, so drying is treated as urgent even when the fire itself was contained.

Can smoke odor really be removed, or will it always come back?

It can be removed, but only by dealing with the sources. Odor comes back when porous material that absorbed smoke (insulation, carpet pad, drywall paper, unsealed framing) is left in place and covered with sealer or a fragrance. Done properly, the contaminated porous material comes out, remaining surfaces are cleaned and treated, ductwork is addressed, and air treatment runs until the smell is gone rather than masked.
