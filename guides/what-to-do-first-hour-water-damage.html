What to Do in the First Hour After a Pipe Bursts or You Find Water Damage

You are standing in water, or you just pulled up a rug and found the pad underneath is soaked, and you have no idea how long it has been like that. Your first thought is probably not a plan. It is the sick feeling of not knowing how bad this is or how much it will cost. That reaction is normal. Water moves fast and quietly, and most homeowners have never had to deal with this before. The next hour matters more than the next week, and the steps below are the ones that actually change the outcome.

What to Do in the First Hour After a Pipe Bursts or You Find Water Damage

Minutes 0–10

Stop the water and cut the risk

Everything else waits until the water stops and the area is safe. Shut off the water. Most Arizona homes have a main shutoff where the supply line enters the house, commonly in a box near the street, in a wall box on the exterior, or in the garage near the water heater. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If the leak is at one fixture (toilet, sink, water heater, washing machine), there is usually a smaller local valve at that fixture you can close instead. If the valve is stuck or corroded, go to the main. If water is near outlets, light fixtures, or a panel, do not walk into it. Cut power to that area at the breaker if you can reach the panel from a dry spot. If you cannot reach it safely, leave the area and call an electrician or your utility. If the water came from a sewer line, a toilet backup, or an unknown source outside the house, treat it as contaminated. Keep kids and pets out. Do not touch it without gloves and boots. If the leak is a water heater and you smell gas, or the heater is gas-fired and now sitting in water, shut off the gas supply to it and leave gas repairs to a licensed technician.

Minutes 10–30

Document before you clean anything

Once the water is off, take pictures and video before you move a single item. Insurance adjusters reconstruct what happened from what you captured in the first half hour. Cleaning up first is the most common and most expensive mistake homeowners make. Capture: wide shots of each affected room, the water line on the walls and baseboards, the actual source of the leak (the pipe, the failed supply hose, the appliance), the ceiling if water came from above, and close-ups of damaged furniture, flooring, and belongings. Get the whole scene, not just the worst spot. Write down the time you found it and the time the water stopped. Save the broken part or hose if it came loose. It is evidence of what failed. Then call your insurance carrier and open a claim. Ask what your policy covers, what your deductible is, and whether they need anything specific documented. You are allowed to choose your own licensed contractor for the repair; you are not required to use a company the carrier suggests.

Minutes 30–60

Move what you can and start airflow

Now start limiting the spread. Water travels along the flattest path and wicks upward into porous material, so damage keeps growing even after the leak stops. Get items up off wet flooring: cardboard boxes, books, papers, electronics, upholstered furniture, and anything wood. Put foil or plastic under furniture legs so stain does not bleed into carpet. Take up small area rugs. They will transfer dye. Pull standing water with a wet/dry vac or a squeegee toward a drain if the water is clean. Do not use a household vacuum. Do not run a wet/dry vac if you have not confirmed power in that area is safe. Open interior doors and closet doors so trapped air can move, and run ceiling fans. In Phoenix, outdoor air is usually dry enough that running the AC helps pull moisture out, but during monsoon humidity, keep windows closed and rely on the AC instead of outside air. What not to do: do not use a heater to "dry it out faster" (heat plus moisture inside a wall accelerates mold growth), do not lift or tear out wet tile or wood flooring yourself, and do not put fans directly on a wall you suspect has sewage water behind it.

After the first hour

What still needs a professional look

Surface dry is not dry. Drywall, framing, insulation, subfloor, and cabinet toe-kicks hold water you cannot see or feel, and mold can begin developing in that hidden moisture within roughly a couple of days under the right conditions. Professional drying uses moisture meters and thermal imaging to find the wet edge, then places air movers and dehumidifiers to dry the structure to a measured target, not just until it feels dry to the touch. Call for help right away if: water came through a ceiling, the source was a sewer line or toilet overflow, the water sat for more than a day, water reached more than one room, it got under hardwood, laminate, or tile, or the wet area includes drywall you cannot open up yourself. Common Questions

Questions people ask us

Where is my water shutoff valve?

Look for a main shutoff where the supply line enters the house, often in an in-ground box near the street or sidewalk, in a wall box on the exterior of the home, or in the garage near the water heater. Individual fixtures like toilets, sinks, and washing machines usually have their own smaller valve nearby. It is worth locating both now, before an emergency, so you are not searching in the dark.

Should I clean up the water before I take photos?

No. Photograph and video everything first: the source of the leak, the water line on walls, and every affected room and item. Adjusters rely on that documentation to evaluate the claim, and once you have cleaned up, the evidence is gone. Stopping the water comes first; photos come immediately after.

Can I just dry it out with fans myself?

Fans help with surface moisture, but they do not dry inside walls, under flooring, or behind cabinets. Water wicks upward into drywall and insulation where it stays wet for days, and hidden moisture is where mold starts. If water reached drywall, subfloor, or more than one room, it should be checked with moisture readings rather than judged by touch.

Does insurance cover a burst pipe?

Coverage depends on your specific policy and what caused the failure, so call your carrier and ask directly about your deductible and what is included. Two things worth knowing: sudden pipe failures are treated differently than long-term seepage or neglected maintenance, and you generally have the right to choose your own licensed contractor for the repair work.
