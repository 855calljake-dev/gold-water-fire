What fire does to a Chandler house

Why the damage keeps moving after the fire stops

Most of what gets ruined in a house fire is not burned. Smoke travels wherever air travels, and in the block-and-stucco and wood-frame homes built across Chandler through the 1980s, 1990s, and the big Ocotillo and south Chandler build-out of the late 1990s and 2000s, that means the whole conditioned envelope. If the HVAC was running when the fire started, soot went through the return, through the ducts, and settled evenly in bedrooms two rooms away from anything that ever saw a flame. Soot is acidic. On brushed nickel fixtures, on the chrome trim of appliances, on marble and unsealed grout, it starts etching within days, and in the dry Chandler air fine ash stays airborne and redepositing far longer than it would somewhere humid. The other half of the problem is water: whatever the fire department used to knock the fire down is now in the drywall, the pad, and the bottom plates, and in a slab-on-grade home with no basement it wicks sideways along the slab and up into walls that never got hot. Fire damage and water damage arrive together in almost every structure fire, which is why they have to be handled together.

Local conditions

What actually starts fires here, and when

Chandler's risk pattern is not the same as a cold-climate city's. Peak load is summer, when air conditioners run nearly continuously against 110-degree afternoons and older condensers, disconnects, and undersized branch circuits fail under sustained draw. Attic temperatures in a Chandler house in July can climb high enough to bake wire insulation and the plastic on old junction-box connections year after year. Monsoon season, roughly July through September, brings lightning and hard voltage swings that take out electronics and sometimes start attic and service-panel fires. Then there is the shoulder-season spike: outdoor cooking, fire pits, and holiday lighting in the cooler months, plus a lot of homes across the East Valley where the original 1990s smoke detectors were never replaced. Detached garages and workshops matter here too, because so many Chandler properties store paint, fuel, and pool chemicals in an uninsulated garage that hits 120 degrees. None of that changes what you do after the fire, but it does explain why we see whole neighborhoods of same-era homes with the same failure points.

How the work goes

The order things have to happen in

There is a sequence to this, and skipping steps is what leaves a house smelling like smoke a year later. First the structure gets secured and stabilized: board-up, roof tarp, power and gas verified, and the wet materials pulled or dried before mold gets a foothold in the drywall. Then contents get sorted, inventoried, and either packed out for cleaning or documented as a loss, because the insurance conversation goes much better when someone wrote it all down at the start. Then comes demolition of unsalvageable material, cleaning of every surface soot touched with the right method for the material, and sealing of framing and subfloor where odor has soaked in. Odor removal is last, not first, because deodorizing a house that still has soot in the wall cavities only masks the smell until the next hot week. After that it is rebuild. Common Questions

Questions people ask us

Can I go back inside my house after a fire?

Only after the fire department or another authority clears the structure. Even then, soot is acidic and fine ash stays airborne, so limit time inside and avoid disturbing surfaces. If you do go in for essentials, grab identification, medication, and important documents, and do not run the HVAC, since that pushes soot through the ducts into rooms that were not affected.

Why does my whole house smell like smoke when the fire was only in one room?

Smoke follows airflow. It moves through doorways, up into the attic, and through the return and supply ducts if the system was running. Odor molecules soak into porous materials, drywall, insulation, carpet pad, framing, and upholstery, and they re-release when the house heats up. That is why odor work happens after all soot residue and affected materials are removed, not before.

Do I need a separate contractor to rebuild after the cleanup?

Not with us. We handle the mitigation and the reconstruction, so the same company that documented the damage puts the house back together. That avoids the gap where a homeowner ends up coordinating between a cleanup crew, a builder, and an adjuster who all have different notes.
